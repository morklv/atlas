# Survey-to-route engineering status

Open `output/swarm/field.html` after `bash run.sh swarm`. It is the primary,
unified survey-and-ground-route page. Its default data is generated by a
synthetic three-drone survey, not a real-world map. The aerial-image workflow
uses five simulated observers sharing one map. It can classify an uploaded
overhead image into roads/tracks, open ground, low vegetation, forest, water
and buildings, then simulate survey evidence over that terrain. It also imports
a local metric XYZ CSV/ASCII PLY point cloud for direct routing.
The older `navigation.html` remains available for its separate experimental
aerial 3D free-space planner and GeoJSON export.
The unified page lets you pick a destination and plan a **candidate** ground
route. The older page also offers a candidate aircraft route over the synthetic
ray-carved voxel volume.
Empty cells remain unknown and are blocked. The ground planner uses maximum
measured surface height, movement limits and clearance; the aerial planner uses
only the simulated ray-carved free-space voxels. An endpoint-only imported cloud
does not establish free air, so aircraft planning is disabled for that import.

The current map answers a specific question: *is there an observed, geometrically
connected corridor from start to destination for this robot size and movement
limit?* It does not certify the corridor as safe. A surface height is not a
terrain label, soil-strength test, traversability classifier or obstacle-motion
prediction. The route does not control a robot. Structural-height differences
are review candidates, not validated damage detections.

## What a real survey would require

1. Define an authorized survey polygon and a known coordinate frame. A pasted
   map URL gives an approximate geographic anchor, **not** terrain, imagery,
   elevation or flight authorization. A single photo cannot recover an accurate
   3D model by itself.
2. Collect overlapping, calibrated camera images or LiDAR scans with timestamped
   poses. A real system needs GNSS/RTK and IMU, visual-inertial odometry or SLAM,
   extrinsic calibration, time synchronization and a consistent local frame.
3. Register multiple views; fuse points into a height/occupancy map; quantify
   coverage and uncertainty. Handle occlusion, moving objects and bad poses.
4. Segment terrain, vegetation and structures where relevant; estimate actual
   ground surface and robot-specific traversability. ATLAS currently runs a
   local Mask2Former aerial segmentation model and has a conservative
   LiDAR-semantic fusion contract. The image model does not measure depth,
   clearance, soil strength or route safety.
5. Plan on observed, fresh evidence with a robot footprint and motion constraints;
   execute through a real autonomy stack; detect changes and replan. Require
   field tests and safety procedures before treating a route as operational.

The browser accepts a photo only as visible context; it does not infer depth or
georeference pixels. An Earth anchor can turn local route coordinates into a
GeoJSON visualization, but without surveyed georeferencing its geographic
accuracy is only as good as the supplied anchor. Vertical datum is unspecified.

## Evidence and next experiment

The ground and air route algorithms, unknown-space rejection, clearance, point import and coordinate mapping have automated tests in `tests/navigation.test.cjs`. The strongest next portfolio improvement is to import a small, properly georeferenced outdoor dataset; document its frame and calibration; hold out ground-truth points; and report map-height or registration error, route validity, latency, and failure cases.
